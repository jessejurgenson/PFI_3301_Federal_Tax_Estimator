How to Use This Calculator

  1. Select the tax year and filing status.
  2. Enter the taxpayer's taxable income sources.
  3. Enter allowable adjustments to income to calculate Adjusted Gross Income (AGI).
  4. Enter common itemized deductions. The calculator will compare the itemized total with the applicable standard deduction and show which is larger.
  5. Enter applicable tax credits, withholding, and estimated payments.
  6. Select Calculate Estimate, then review the calculation trail and tax-by-bracket table.

For class problems, enter only the information provided unless your instructor directs you otherwise.

Common Mistakes
  • Applying the highest marginal tax rate to all taxable income.
  • Subtracting 401(k), 403(b), Health FSA, Dependent Care FSA, or similar payroll reductions a second time when they are already reflected in federal taxable wages.
  • Treating a tax deduction like a tax credit.
  • Assuming federal income tax withholding equals final tax liability.
  • Entering a full mortgage payment instead of deductible mortgage interest.
  • Counting property taxes twice within State and Local Taxes (SALT).
  • Assuming all medical expenses are deductible.
Tax Calculation FAQs

Use these frequently asked questions to review the major concepts behind the calculator.

Gross Income → subtract adjustments → Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) → subtract standard or itemized deduction → Taxable Income → apply tax brackets → subtract credits → Tax Liability
Gross Income vs. Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)

Gross income is taxable income before adjustments. Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) is gross income reduced by allowable adjustments to income, sometimes called above-the-line deductions.

Standard Deduction vs. Itemized Deductions

The standard deduction is a fixed amount based mainly on filing status. Itemized deductions are the total of qualifying deductible expenses, such as allowable mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable contributions, and certain medical expenses. The calculator compares the two and identifies the larger deduction.

Are home property taxes deductible?

Allowable state and local real property taxes on a home can generally be included as an itemized deduction within State and Local Taxes (SALT). Property tax amounts should not be entered twice. Certain assessments for local improvements or specific services are not treated the same as deductible real property taxes.

Marginal Tax Rate vs. Effective Tax Rate

Marginal tax rate is the rate on the next dollar of ordinary taxable income. Effective tax rate is estimated tax liability divided by taxable income.

Tax Credits vs. Tax Deductions

A deduction reduces income subject to tax. A credit reduces tax liability directly. Nonrefundable credits cannot reduce income tax below $0 in this simplified estimator; refundable credits are treated as payments.

How are 401(k) and 403(b) contributions treated?

Traditional 401(k) and 403(b) employee contributions: These elective deferrals are generally made before federal income tax and are usually already excluded from Form W-2 Box 1 wages. If the case gives you Box 1 wages, do not subtract the contribution again.

Roth 401(k) and Roth 403(b) contributions: These are made after tax and are generally included in Form W-2 Box 1 wages, so they do not reduce current federal taxable income.

Classroom rule: Use the federal taxable wage amount provided. Do not subtract traditional workplace retirement contributions again when the tax benefit is already reflected in the wage figure.
How are Health FSA and Dependent Care FSA contributions treated?

Flexible Spending Arrangement (FSA) contributions made through a qualifying employer cafeteria plan are generally handled through payroll before federal taxable wages are reported. Because this calculator asks for federal taxable wages, usually Form W-2 Box 1, do not enter those contributions again as an adjustment to income.

Health Flexible Spending Arrangement (Health FSA): Employee salary-reduction limits are $3,300 for 2025 and $3,400 for 2026.

Dependent Care Flexible Spending Arrangement (Dependent Care FSA): The general exclusion limit is $5,000 for 2025 ($2,500 if Married Filing Separately) and $7,500 for 2026 ($3,750 if Married Filing Separately), subject to earned-income and plan rules.

Classroom rule: If a case gives Form W-2 Box 1 wages, the FSA tax benefit is generally already reflected in that number. Subtracting the contribution again would double-count the tax benefit.
Refund vs. Additional Tax Due

A refund occurs when withholding, estimated payments, and refundable credits exceed tax liability. Additional tax is due when liability exceeds those payments. A refund does not mean the taxpayer had no tax liability.

What is the difference between filing statuses?

Filing status affects the standard deduction, tax brackets, filing requirements, and eligibility for some deductions and credits. It is generally based on your marital and household situation at the end of the tax year.

Single: Generally used if you are unmarried, divorced, or legally separated at the end of the year and do not qualify for another status such as Head of Household.

Married Filing Jointly (MFJ): Married spouses file one return reporting their combined income, deductions, and credits. For many married couples, this status produces a lower combined federal income tax than filing separately.

Married Filing Separately (MFS): Married spouses file separate returns. Each spouse reports their own income, deductions, and credits, subject to special rules. Filing separately can limit or eliminate eligibility for some tax benefits.

Head of Household (HOH): Generally available to an unmarried taxpayer, or certain taxpayers considered unmarried, who paid more than half the cost of maintaining a home for a qualifying person. It typically provides a larger standard deduction and wider tax brackets than Single status.

Qualifying Surviving Spouse: May be available for up to two years after the year a spouse dies if the taxpayer has a qualifying dependent child and meets the other requirements. This calculator does not currently model this filing status.

Why this matters: Two taxpayers with the same income can owe different amounts of federal income tax if their filing statuses are different because the standard deduction and tax-bracket thresholds can differ.
